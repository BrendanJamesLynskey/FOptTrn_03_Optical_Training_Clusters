01

The Question for a Cluster, and the Short Answer

Deck 01 counted the transform work in a training step; deck 02 looked at how optics trains itself. This deck asks the systems question: if every accelerator in a training cluster had a Fourier-optical transform engine beside it (FOptInf 01 for the physics), what would a training step look like, and would it be shorter?

The ceiling

  • For a Llama-3-8B-shaped Hyena-2 model, the part a once-per-step mask could hold is 0.13–0.18% of a step (2,048 to 32,768 tokens): an Amdahl bound of 1.001–1.002x.
  • With block-circulant weights (speculative at this scale) it is 9.53–10.07%: a bound of 1.105–1.112x.

The floor under it

  • Weights change every optimizer step, so every step rewrites every mask: 0.52–2.10 s of frames on the illustrative device, against digital steps of 2.05 s (Hyena-2) and 0.15 s (block-circulant).
  • The weight gradient's Fourier-domain products would need a new mask per sequence: minutes per step on one device, about a whole digital step even spread over 64 engines (slide 08). They stay digital.

Short answer: at today's mask rates the engine lengthens the step rather than shortening it, and the frequency transform that genuinely helps a training cluster (DeMo's DCT of the momentum) is worth link bytes, not FLOPs. All numbers come from results.md, written by analysis/train_flop_share.py; the step size (1,048,576 tokens), the cluster (64 GPUs at 400 TFLOP/s sustained) and the device (2-megapixel DMD at 1,031 Hz) are illustrative.

02

Anatomy of a Training Step

One optimizer step processes a global batch (here 1,048,576 tokens) and ends with a single weight update. Four phases, of which only the first two contain transforms:

PhaseWhat it doesWhat it costsTransform work?
ForwardEvery layer on every sequence; activations kept (or checkpointed) for the backwardAbout 2 FLOPs per parameter per tokenLong convolutions: FFT, times H, IFFT
BackwardActivation gradients (dX) and weight gradients (dW), layer by layer in reverseAbout twice the forward: the 6N ruleFFT of G; dU = IFFT(G·conj(H)); dH += G·conj(U)
Gradient syncData-parallel replicas sum their gradients: an all-reduce, or a reduce-scatter and all-gather when optimizer state is sharded (ZeRO, Rajbhandari et al., arXiv:1910.02054)Bytes on the network, proportional to the parametersNone, unless the gradients are compressed (slide 11)
OptimizerAdamW's element-wise update of weights and momentsMemory-bound element-wise work (not counted)None for AdamW; small DCTs for DeMo

The FLOP ledger is checked against the 6N rule: for the transformer variant it gives 46.64 GFLOP per token at 2,048 tokens and 70.80 at 32,768, a ratio of 1.0000 to 6N plus the attention term (results.md §1).

What is new against inference

The weights change at the end of every step. An inference engine's Fourier-plane masks hold the same filter spectra for the life of a deployment; a training engine's masks are stale after each update and must be written again before the next step's forward pass.

03

Parallelism, and Where the Engine Sits

Suppose each accelerator has a transform engine beside it that takes the FFTs and weight-masked products of the layers that accelerator owns. What each engine's mask must hold depends on how the model is split:

ParallelismWhat it splitsEach engine's mask holds
DataThe batch, across replicasThe whole model's spectra, in every replica: mask writes are replicated, not divided
Tensor (Megatron-LM, Shoeybi et al., arXiv:1909.08053)Each layer's matrices across GPUsA slice of each layer; Hyena's per-channel long convolutions split by channel with no extra communication
PipelineLayers into stagesOnly its stage's layers
Sequence / contextThe sequence dimensionA long convolution needs the whole sequence: splitting it turns one FFT into a distributed FFT with an all-to-all

Megatron's own sequence parallelism splits only the norm and dropout regions along the sequence (Korthikanti et al., arXiv:2205.05198), which a long convolution would tolerate; splitting the convolution itself would not suit an engine that transforms whole rows at once.

04

Masks Change Every Step

A 2-megapixel mask holds a small fraction of the model's filter spectra (FOptInf 02, “Fourier-Plane Mask Capacity”), so an engine cycles through frames as it walks the layers. In inference that cycle serves each batch with fixed weights. In training the weights are new every step, and the backward pass needs them again:

VariantSequenceSequences / stepDigital step timeWeight-mask frames (fwd + bwd)Time
Hyena-22,0485122.05 s5380.52 s
Hyena-28,1921282.05 s2,1482.08 s
Hyena-2 + block-circulant weights2,0485120.15 s5540.54 s
Hyena-2 + block-circulant weights8,1921280.15 s2,1642.10 s

Source: results.md, section “4. Holding the masks”. Illustrative: 2-megapixel DMD at 1,031 Hz, one complex value per pixel (optimistic); 64 GPUs at 400 TFLOP/s; layer-major order.

  • The mask bill rivals the whole step. For Hyena-2 it is a quarter of the digital step at 2,048 tokens and about all of it at 8,192, to remove 0.13–0.15% of the FLOPs. For block-circulant weights the step is 0.15 s and the masks take 0.54–2.10 s.
  • Longer sequences cost more frames, because a filter spectrum has a bin per padded sample; fewer sequences per step does not reduce the count, since every layer's spectra are written once per step regardless.
  • The backward does not strictly need a separate conj(H) mask: for a real filter, correlating with h is convolving the time-reversed gradient with h and reversing the result. But by the time the backward reaches a layer the mask holds other layers, so its spectra are written again either way; the table counts both writes.
  • Compare inference, where the same device writes 269 frames per forward pass of a 2,048-token Hyena-2 prompt (FOptInf 02) and the simulator found mask rewrites dominate the engine's time (FOptInf 03, “Where the Time Goes”). Training doubles the frames and removes any hope of writing them once.

The model, stated plainly. Digital step = per-token step FLOPs × tokens ÷ (GPUs × TFLOP/s), with GPU time proportional to FLOPs (FFTs at the matmul rate). Removable = static share × step: the engine does the static work in zero time. Each engine holds the masks of 1/k of the model (k = tensor × pipeline degree) and writes them twice per pass (forward and backward): 2 × ⌈values ÷ (k × pixels)⌉ frames. Layer-major makes one pass per step; sequence-major makes one per microbatch of m sequences on each of the GPUs ÷ k replicas. Not overlapped (conservative): step − removable + mask time. Fully overlapped: the larger of (step − removable) and mask time. Per-token FLOPs and shares are those at 1,048,576 tokens, held fixed for other step sizes (an approximation: the once-per-step filter transforms shift them slightly at long sequences). The data-by-data figure is what writing the weight gradient's conj(U) masks would cost, spread evenly over all engines.

06

Layer-Major Scheduling Against the Pipeline

Layer-major order passes every sequence of the step through one layer, with that layer's spectra on the mask, before moving on: one write per layer per direction per step. It is the order the slide 04 numbers assume, and the opposite of how large models are trained.

What the mask wants

  • All sequences through one layer, then the next.
  • Mask writes amortised over the whole step.
  • But every layer's activations for the whole step must wait for the backward: 1,048,576 tokens' worth, far beyond accelerator memory without recomputation or offload.

What pipelines and memory want

  • GPipe (Huang et al., arXiv:1811.06965) splits the batch into m microbatches that flow through p stages; the idle “bubble” shrinks as m grows.
  • 1F1B, as used in Megatron-LM (Narayanan et al., arXiv:2104.04473), alternates one forward and one backward microbatch per stage, so a stage holds at most about p microbatches' activations; that paper gives the bubble fraction as (p−1)/m.
ScheduleMask passes per step, per engineActivations held
Layer-major, whole step1 (forward) + 1 (backward)The whole step's
GPipe, m microbatchesm forward + m backwardAll m microbatches per stage (less with recomputation)
1F1B, m microbatchesm forward + m backward, interleaved: the mask flips between H and conj H every microbatchAbout p microbatches per stage

Pipeline parallelism does help in one way: a stage's engine holds only its stage's layers, so each pass has fewer frames (the model-parallel degree in the explorer). It does not help enough: for Hyena-2 at 2,048 tokens even one of 32 stages has more spectra than a 2-megapixel mask holds, so each microbatch still costs frames.

07

Gradient Accumulation as the Compromise

Gradient accumulation already splits an optimizer step into rounds whose gradients are summed before one update. Run each round layer-major over its sequences and the trade is explicit:

a rounds per step → mask writes × a → activations ÷ a
  • In the explorer, sequence-major with microbatch m is exactly this, with a = sequences per replica ÷ m. At the defaults (512 sequences of 2,048 tokens over 64 GPUs, 8 per GPU) m = 4 doubles the mask time and m = 1 multiplies it by eight; the weights are the same throughout the step, so only capacity forces the rewrites.
  • Activation checkpointing (Chen et al., arXiv:1604.06174) is the other memory lever. It recomputes the forward during the backward, which for this engine means repeating the forward FFTs and, unless the recomputed layer's spectra are still on the mask, another mask pass. The analysis does not count recomputation, so the static shares here are slightly optimistic for a checkpointed run.
  • Where the compromise lands: whatever a, the engine needs the mask time to hide behind digital work it cannot itself remove. The explorer's “fully overlapped” case shows the best this can do.
A digital accelerator has no such tension

A GPU reads each layer's weights from HBM as it needs them and can switch layers between kernels. The mask is a weight store that changes only 1,031 times a second on the illustrative device, which is why scheduling, not FLOPs, decides this engine's fate in training.

08

Data-by-Data Products Stay Digital

The filter gradient dH = IFFT(Σ G·conj(U)) multiplies two spectra that both depend on the sequence (the gradient of a convolution is a correlation). With block-circulant weights the weight gradient conj(X)·G depends on every token. Either way, a mask would carry data, and would be new for every sequence or token:

Source: results.md, sections 2 and 4 (illustrative device as before).

  • Mask writes that cost more than the work they remove. On one device the data-by-data writes take minutes per step. Spread over 64 engines (the explorer's last tile) the Hyena-2 figure is still about one whole digital step, to remove about 2% of an optical share that is itself 0.14% of the step; the block-circulant figure is many digital steps.
  • So the static share is the ceiling, not the full optical share: for block-circulant weights 9.66% at 4,096 tokens rather than 12.19%.
  • Speculation: a classical joint transform correlator computes the correlation of two inputs without a mask, by placing both in the input plane and transforming the detected intensity of their joint spectrum again. It moves the problem to the input modulator's frame rate and adds a square-law detection and a second pass. Not modelled here.

The Static Amdahl Bound for a Step

Static share = FFTs plus the weight-masked products, over all step FLOPs: what an engine with once-per-step masks could take (glossary). If that work became free, the step would be at most 1/(1−share) faster.

Transformer (Llama-3-8B)any0.00%0.00%0.00%1.000x

  • Training does not rescue the share. The backward has as many FFTs as the forward but twice the matmuls, so the step share sits below the forward share in every row.
  • The bound is a FLOP bound. It assumes the engine and its masks cost no time and that GPUs run FFTs at their matmul rate. If GPUs ran FFTs more slowly (GPU FFT efficiency), the time share would be larger; the analysis does not quantify that for training.
10

What 1.1x Means at Cluster Scale

A bound of 1.105–1.112x is not nothing at cluster scale: it is roughly a tenth of a training run's GPU-hours, which for a large run is a large bill. That is the case for writing it down. The case against is everything the bound leaves out:

What the 1.1x needs

  • A model with block-circulant weights throughout, unproven at LLM scale (FOptInf 02, “Structured Weights”). For Hyena-2 the bound is 1.001–1.002x.
  • Mask writes fully hidden: at the illustrative rate they are 0.54–2.10 s against a 0.15 s digital step.
  • No time lost to conversions, precision passes or moving data to and from the engine.

What it costs

  • One engine per accelerator, because data parallelism replicates the masks: the engine count grows with the cluster while each removes the same fixed share.
  • Static power for the whole run: lasers, thermal tuning and mask drivers draw power whether or not work arrives. In the inference simulator the break-even static power was a few watts against GPUs that run FFTs well and tens of watts against GPUs that do not (FOptInf 03, “Static Power and Energy per Token”).
  • Area, cost and failure modes added beside every GPU (FOptInf 03, “Area, Cost and Performance per Dollar”).
The comparison that matters

At most a tenth of the GPU time, for the most transform-heavy and least established model, against an extra optical subsystem on every accelerator. The same tenth could be bought by adding about a tenth more GPUs, with no new model architecture, no mask schedule and no analogue precision budget.

The Optimizer Step and DeMo

AdamW's update is element-wise and memory-bound: no transforms. One recent optimizer does use them. DeMo (Peng, Quesnelle, Kingma, “DeMo: Decoupled Momentum Optimization”, arXiv:2411.19870, 2024) lets each worker keep its own momentum, takes a DCT of it in small chunks, sends only the largest coefficients (top-k) to the other workers, and removes what it sent from its local momentum. The slow components stay local, decoupled from the exchange.

ParametersDeMo transform FLOPs per stepStep FLOPs (transformer, 4,096)Share
8,029,995,0082.409e+115.059e+164.76e-06

Source: results.md, section “3. The optimizer step and DeMo”: a DCT and an inverse DCT per chunk of 64, at the real-FFT cost.

  • As compute, it is nothing. Five millionths of the step, in 64-point transforms: far too small and too short for an optical engine, whose conversions and passes cost more per value than a GPU spends on a 64-point DCT.
  • As communication, it is the point. What DeMo removes is gradient bytes on the network between replicas, the phase of the step (slide 02) that no transform engine touches.
  • So the one place frequency transforms clearly earn their keep in a training cluster is the link, which is the subject of the Compute in Transit series, not of a compute engine.
12

Frequency Transforms in Transit (Speculative)

Gradient compression is an established line of work: sparsification (Deep Gradient Compression, Lin et al., arXiv:1712.01887), low-rank updates (PowerSGD, Vogels et al., arXiv:1905.13727) and DeMo's DCT. All of them compress at the worker, on the accelerator. The speculative question is whether a frequency transform could run in the link's data path, on traffic that has to move anyway.

gradients → transform + keep top-k (in the NIC or link) → fewer bytes → sum, inverse at the receiver
  • Why it is plausible: a DCT is linear, so the sum of transformed gradients is the transform of the summed gradient; aggregation could in principle happen in the transform domain.
  • Why it is hard: each worker's top-k picks different coefficients, so sparse contributions do not line up for an in-place sum and must be gathered instead; and the optimizer's error feedback (DeMo keeps what it did not send) needs state at the worker, not in the link.
  • Optics here would be photonic interconnect, moving the bytes, not a Fourier engine computing on them (glossary). The inference series asked the same question for the KV hand-off and for collectives (FOptInf 03, “Beyond Compression”).
Speculation

Nothing on this slide is measured. Whether in-transit frequency compression of gradients preserves convergence, and at what line rate it could run, is open; the Compute in Transit series takes it up. No optical-computing company is described here; the mapping is the author's own.

And the costs that apply even where it maps: a mask rewritten every step, conversions per value, static power on every accelerator for the whole run, and one engine per data-parallel replica because the masks are replicated.

14

What to Take Away

For this deck and for the series:

  • Transforms are a small share of training, as of inference. A Llama-3-8B-shaped Hyena-2 model spends 0.13–0.18% of a step on work a once-per-step mask could hold (bound 1.002x at most); only speculative block-circulant weights reach 9.53–10.07% (1.105–1.112x).
  • Training adds two costs inference does not have. Masks must be rewritten every optimizer step, 0.52–2.10 s on the illustrative device against digital steps of 0.15–2.05 s, and the weight gradient's data-by-data products would need a mask per sequence, minutes per step, so they stay digital.
  • In a cluster, scheduling decides it. Only layer-major order amortises the writes, and it fights pipelines and activation memory; data parallelism replicates the masks on every engine. Even fully hidden, a free engine buys at most about a tenth of the GPU time, against static power and an optical subsystem per accelerator.
  • Where frequency transforms do help training is the link. DeMo's DCTs are 4.76e-06 of a step's FLOPs and save gradient bytes: a communication result, not a compute one.
